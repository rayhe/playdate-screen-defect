Federal Legal Context

  • Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act (15 U.S.C. 2301-2312): Panic provides a written "Limited Warranty" on the Playdate, a consumer product exceeding $25. Under Magnuson-Moss, the implied warranty of merchantability cannot be disclaimed. Denying warranty claims by asserting an exclusion (impact damage) without evidence that the exclusion applies may violate the Act's consumer protections.
  • FTC Enforcement Precedent: In April 2018, the FTC sent warning letters to Sony, Microsoft, Nintendo, HTC, ASUS, and others about predatory warranty practices violating the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, stating such practices "may result in legal action." While those letters addressed repair restrictions specifically, they demonstrate the FTC's active enforcement of Magnuson-Moss against consumer electronics companies that use warranty exclusions to deny valid claims.

Complaint Description

Panic, Inc. manufactures the Playdate, a $229 handheld game console. I purchased one that developed a progressive screen defect while sitting on my desk on a charger. The device was never dropped, struck, or impacted. I am the only person who used it.

On June 8, 2026, I contacted Panic support with photos documenting the defect. On July 30, 2026, support representative Amy Forbes approved a warranty replacement (RMA) and sent a prepaid shipping label. I shipped the unit back.

On August 14, 2026, after receiving my unit, Panic reversed the warranty approval, claiming "impact damage." They offered to return the unit at my expense or provide a 30% discount coupon. They did not offer a replacement.

On August 18, 2026, Amy wrote:

"I approved the RMA because you claimed the unit had not been damaged, but as the photo shows, there has been impact damage to the screen at some point."

She also admitted:

"I was trying to solicit more information without the implication that you were not being truthful. We have had many customers claim that nothing has ever happened to their Playdate, we approve the RMA, then, once it arrives, we see that there was an undisclosed non-manufacturer's issue with the unit."

Why the "Impact Damage" Diagnosis Is Unsupported

The defect changed between my pre-shipping photos and Panic's post-receipt photo. Before shipping: dense vertical line artifacts across the full screen. After Panic received it: a localized dark diagonal streak in the lower left. Impact damage creates a fixed pattern at the moment of contact. A defect that transforms over weeks is progressive LCD failure, not impact.

There is no physical evidence of impact: no cracking, no case damage, no identifiable point of contact anywhere on the device. The Playdate's display is glued directly to the housing with no protective frame (iFixit teardown), a construction known to be susceptible to adhesive degradation.

Panic produced no diagnostic report, no technician assessment, and identified no specific point of impact. The denial rests on a visual impression and a generalized assumption that customers misrepresent how they handled their devices.

The Playdate's design also violates two separate specifications from Sharp's own datasheet for the display panel (LS027B7DH01): one warning against placing adhesive on the panel surface, and another limiting static image display to two hours. The Playdate bonds the LCD to the housing with adhesive and displays a static clock face indefinitely while charging, creating conditions for the exact type of progressive LCD failure documented here. These design nonconformities were not disclosed to consumers.

Why This Matters Beyond One Unit

I am not filing this complaint to recover the cost of my unit. I am filing because Amy's admission about "many customers" suggests Panic routinely denies warranty claims using the same unsupported "impact damage" classification. Customers who lack the technical knowledge to distinguish progressive LCD failure from impact damage are being denied coverage based on an assumption, not a diagnosis.

The FTC has demonstrated its willingness to enforce Magnuson-Moss against consumer electronics companies. Panic's practice of asserting damage exclusions without diagnostic evidence raises the same consumer protection concerns those letters addressed.
